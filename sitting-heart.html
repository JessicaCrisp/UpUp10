What sitting all day does to your heart — and why exercise alone isn't enough

The research on prolonged sitting and cardiovascular health has sharpened considerably in the last two years. Here's what it shows — and what actually helps.

JC
Jess Crisp Founder, UpUp10 · 23x World Champion · 4x Olympian · Sydney

If you exercise regularly and still feel like your health is quietly slipping — energy dropping, weight creeping up, something just not right — there may be a reason that has nothing to do with how hard you're working out.

The research on prolonged sitting and cardiovascular health has sharpened considerably in the last two years. What it shows is specific, and a little uncomfortable for people who thought a daily workout was enough.

The study that changed the conversation

In November 2024, researchers at Mass General Brigham published a study in the Journal of the American College of Cardiology that tracked 89,530 people using accelerometers — actual movement data, not self-reported estimates.1 The finding: when daily sedentary time exceeded 10.6 hours, the risk of heart failure and cardiovascular death climbed by 40 to 60 percent.

That number would be striking on its own. What made the study land differently was the second finding: that risk held even in people who were physically active and meeting the recommended 150 minutes of weekly exercise.

"Sedentary risk remained even in people who were physically active, which is important because many of us sit a lot and think that if we can get out at the end of the day and do some exercise we can counterbalance it."

— Dr. Ezimamaka Ajufo, lead researcher, Mass General Brigham, 2024

The exercise isn't the problem. The assumption that it cancels out everything else is.

Why exercise can't undo sitting

The biology explains why. When you sit for extended periods, three things happen in the body that a later workout cannot fully reverse.

Muscle contractions drop to near zero. Your large leg muscles — the ones that are most metabolically active and most responsible for moving glucose out of the bloodstream — go almost entirely quiet. The longer they stay quiet, the more the body's glucose metabolism slows. A workout later in the day uses those muscles, but it doesn't retroactively address the hours they spent inactive.

Blood flow slows. Circulation through the lower body decreases during prolonged sitting, which affects how efficiently the cardiovascular system is working across the whole day — not just during exercise.

Insulin sensitivity decreases. The more hours you spend sedentary, the less effectively your cells respond to insulin. This is one of the pathways through which long-term sitting raises cardiovascular risk, and it accumulates across the hours of the day regardless of what you do at either end of it.

Andrea LaCroix, an epidemiologist at UC San Diego who studies sedentary behavior, put it plainly: "If I take a brisk long walk for an hour but sit the rest of the day, I'm still accruing all the negative effects on my metabolism."2 The walk fills in one column. The sitting fills in another. They don't cancel.

It affects younger people too

A 2024 study from UC Riverside, published in PLOS One, looked specifically at adults in their 20s and 30s — people who don't usually worry about cardiovascular risk.3 Using pairs of identical twins to control for genetics, the researchers found that sitting eight or more hours a day raised cholesterol ratios and BMI even in physically active participants.

Their conclusion was direct: the standard exercise guidelines — 150 minutes of moderate activity per week — were not enough to offset the effects of that much sitting. To meaningfully lower the risk, people needed to roughly double the recommended exercise. Or, more practically: sit less.

How much are most desk workers actually sitting?

More than they estimate. A 2025 US Bureau of Labor Statistics survey found that software developers spend 97% of their working day sitting. Marketing managers: 93%. Lawyers: 86%.4 Australian research tracking office workers across all hours — desk time, commute, and evening — put the median at nine hours of daily sitting.5 For people working from home, the number tends to be higher because the commute disappears but the chair doesn't.

The threshold where cardiovascular risk climbs sharply — 10.6 hours — is closer to an ordinary Tuesday than most people realise.

What the research says about fixing it

In 2025, the British Journal of Sports Medicine published findings from what's now called the Body Electric Study — one of the largest real-world experiments on movement breaks ever conducted.6 Columbia University researchers recruited 19,342 adults through NPR and ran a two-week experiment, assigning participants to take five-minute walking breaks every 30, 60, or 120 minutes throughout their day.

Across all three groups: fatigue fell, mood improved, and afternoon energy increased. None of the groups reported that breaks hurt their productivity — in fact, all three reported higher work engagement after two weeks.

The 30-minute group got the best outcomes but found the schedule genuinely demanding. The two-hour group found it easy but got less benefit. Hourly breaks — five minutes of walking per hour — came out as the practical sweet spot: real improvement, realistic to sustain.

Five minutes per hour is roughly 3% of a working day. The return on that investment, in the research at least, is significant.

What you can actually do about it

The goal isn't to eliminate sitting — it's to break it up regularly throughout the day. The challenge is that nothing in a standard desk setup reminds you to do this. The work certainly doesn't. So the real problem to solve is: how do you make sure you actually get up?

Attach it to something that already happens.

The most reliable way to build any new habit is to connect it to something you already do consistently. Finishing a task before opening the next one is a natural moment — stand up between tasks, even for thirty seconds, before you switch. Making coffee works too. The trigger needs to be something that happens roughly once an hour without you having to decide to do it.

Set a recurring hourly alarm.

A phone alarm set to repeat every hour during work hours is simple and it works. The key is not dismissing it and sitting straight back down — actually stand up, move around your space for a few minutes, then return. Name the alarm something that makes you take it seriously.

When you get up, use your legs.

This is the part that matters biologically. The large leg muscles — quadriceps, hamstrings, glutes — go almost entirely quiet during sitting, and their inactivity is the main driver of the cardiovascular effects described above. Walking around your space, climbing a flight of stairs, doing a few slow squats: anything that gets those muscles contracting again. The movement doesn't need to be intense. Two minutes of walking is more effective than two minutes of standing still.

Start smaller than you think you need to.

One reliable break per day is worth more than an ambitious hourly schedule you abandon by Wednesday. Build the frequency gradually — even two or three breaks a day is a meaningful reduction in unbroken sitting time. The Body Electric research showed benefit at every frequency tested. More is better, but anything is better than nothing.

For the reminder itself, a recurring phone alarm is genuinely sufficient. If you want something that handles it automatically and gives you a short guided movement when it fires, UpUp10 is a free Mac app built for exactly this. But the tool matters far less than the decision that regular breaks are a non-negotiable part of your workday.

The thing most people get wrong

The instinct when you learn about sitting's cardiovascular effects is to compensate — more gym time, a longer run, a standing desk. These things aren't useless, but they're addressing the wrong variable.

Standing desks, for what it's worth, have the same problem if you simply swap one static posture for another. The research on them is mixed precisely because standing all day produces its own cardiovascular and musculoskeletal strain. What the body needs isn't a different position. It's movement, regularly, throughout the day.

I spent 30 years as a competitive athlete and thought I understood physical health better than most. Three months at a desk humbled me. What fixed it wasn't training harder. It was understanding that the sitting itself was a separate problem from fitness — one that needed its own solution, built into the hours between 9 and 5, not bolted on afterwards.

Small, frequent movement breaks compound over a working life in ways a gym session can't replicate on its own. You don't need to exercise more. You need to stop sitting for so long without getting up. That's it. Everything else is just making that easier to remember.
