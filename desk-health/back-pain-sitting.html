Back pain from sitting: what's actually causing it and how to undo it

Most desk-related back pain isn't a posture problem. It isn't a furniture problem. It's a movement problem — and the fix doesn't require buying anything.

You've probably been told to sit up straight. Maybe you've bought a lumbar support, adjusted your monitor height, or tried a standing desk. And yet the ache at the base of your spine keeps coming back. That's because these solutions address the symptom, not the cause.

The cause is simpler and harder to fix at the same time: your body was not built to hold still for hours at a stretch. Here's what's actually happening inside when you do.

Three things that go wrong when you sit for too long

1. Your hip flexors shorten

The iliopsoas — the deep muscle connecting your lower spine to your thigh — sits in a shortened position every minute you're in a chair. Over time, it adapts. Research published in the Journal of Orthopaedic and Sports Physical Therapy found that after just 60 minutes of continuous sitting, hip flexor flexibility is measurably reduced compared to baseline — and these muscles do not return to their normal length without active stretching or movement.

Source: Journal of Orthopaedic and Sports Physical Therapy, via Stealth Agents Research 2026

Tight hip flexors pull your pelvis forward. That tilt flattens your lumbar curve, increases strain on the lower back muscles, and reduces glute activation — the very muscles meant to support your spine. A study of 512 lower back pain patients found that hip flexor tightness was associated with limited lumbar mobility in all directions, and that prolonged sitting was a primary driver of that tightness.

Source: Ekabutr et al., Journal of Health Science of Thailand, 2023

2. Your spinal discs go under sustained compression

When you stand, your intervertebral discs distribute weight relatively evenly. When you sit without lumbar support, your spine flexes forward and disc pressure increases significantly. Research has consistently found that unsupported sitting places higher loads on the lumbar discs than standing — and that slouched sitting pushes those loads even higher.

Source: XChair: The Science of Sitting; Li et al., Life, 2022

MRI studies found measurable disc height loss in the lower lumbar region after just four hours of continuous sitting. The catch: when participants followed a simple protocol of standing and stretching every 15 minutes, disc height remained stable. No damage occurred.

Source: Workspace.ae: Lower Back Pain From Sitting — What's Really Happening to Your Spine
The key finding: it's not sitting itself that causes damage — it's sustained static sitting. Your body needs position changes to stay healthy. The problem isn't the chair. It's the stillness.

3. Your back muscles stop firing correctly

Research from Memorial University of Newfoundland found that after extended sitting, the timing of back muscle responses changes — muscles that normally activate quickly to protect the spine are delayed. That lag increases injury risk. Prolonged sitting also allows the glutes to essentially "switch off," shifting the stabilisation load back onto muscles that weren't designed for it.

Source: Dr. De Carvalho, Memorial University Biomechanics Laboratory

What actually undoes it

The good news: the body adapts in both directions. The same adaptability that lets your hip flexors shorten also lets them lengthen again — if you give them the right inputs consistently. Here are four things you can do right now, and every day.

  1. 1 Half-kneeling hip flexor stretch. Kneel on one knee with your opposite foot forward, both knees at 90 degrees. Tuck your pelvis gently under (don't arch your back) and shift your weight forward until you feel a stretch deep in the front of the back hip. Hold 30–45 seconds each side. Do this every time you stand up from your desk. A daily lunge-and-reach stretch has been shown to produce measurable improvement in hip flexor length over six weeks, along with improved glute activation.
  2. 2 Glute activation — the bridge. Lie on your back, knees bent, feet flat. Drive your hips to the ceiling by squeezing your glutes. Hold for 2 seconds at the top. Do 10–15 reps. This directly counteracts the glute inhibition caused by hours of sitting. It takes 90 seconds and requires no equipment.
  3. 3 Thoracic rotation. Sit on the edge of your chair. Cross your arms over your chest. Rotate your upper body slowly left, then right, as far as comfortable. Eight rotations each direction. This restores movement to the thoracic spine — the upper and mid back — which stiffens when you're hunched over a screen and compensates by overloading the lower back.
  4. 4 Stand up and walk for 2 minutes. Not because you need to go anywhere. Because a 2023 randomised controlled trial in the Journal of Physical Therapy Science found that 2-minute standing or mobility breaks every 30 minutes reduced lumbar strain by 55% over 12 weeks. Two minutes. Every 30 minutes. That's all it takes to interrupt the damage cycle.
Sources: Sensai Fit: Hip Mobility Research Review; Journal of Physical Therapy Science, 2023 RCT

The real problem: you forget to move

You already know you should move more. Everyone does. The research above isn't new — it's been published, cited, and ignored for decades. That's because knowing isn't the same as doing, and doing requires remembering, and remembering requires a trigger that isn't your own willpower.

This is where a movement reminder changes everything — not because it teaches you new exercises, but because it turns an intention you already have into a behaviour that actually happens.

The habit research on this is clear: people who tie new behaviours to existing triggers are far more likely to maintain them than people who rely on motivation alone. A reminder that fires while you're already at your desk is a more effective trigger than a gym membership you drive to.

For back pain specifically, the consistency of interruption matters more than the duration of movement. Ten seconds of standing and stretching, every hour, every day, outperforms a 30-minute yoga class twice a week — because it addresses the root cause of the problem as it's happening.
